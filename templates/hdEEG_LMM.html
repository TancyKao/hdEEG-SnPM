📊 EEG Analysis Methods: Comprehensive Summary

Study Reference

Title: Conscious experiences and high-density EEG patterns predicting subjective sleep depth

Authors: Stephan et al., 2021

Journal: Current Biology 31, 1–14, December 20, 2021

DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2021.10.012

🎯 Study Overview

Research Question

What accounts for feeling deeply asleep? The study challenged the notion that slow-wave sleep best accounts for feeling deeply asleep.

Participants

  • Good sleepers: n = 20 (age 38.3 ± 7.4 years, 15 females)
  • Sleep misperceptors: n = 10 (age 40.8 ± 6.5 years, 8 females)
  • Total awakenings: 787 (26.7 ± 7.1 per good sleeper, 25.3 ± 5.7 per misperceptor)

Key Findings

  • Subjective sleep depth was lowest in early NREM sleep (first 2 hours)
  • Subjective sleep depth was highest in REM sleep (good sleepers)
  • High-frequency EEG activity (beta, gamma) predicted lighter subjective sleep
  • Sleep misperceptors showed abnormally low REM sleep depth
  • Dream-like experiences correlated with deeper subjective sleep

🔬 EEG Data Analysis Pipeline

1. Data Acquisition & Preprocessing

Hardware

  • 256-channel high-density EEG system (Electrical Geodesics)
  • Sampling rate: 500 Hz
  • ~248 cortical channels (excluding EOG/EMG electrodes)

Preprocessing Steps

  1. Band-pass filtering: 0.5–45 Hz
  2. Sleep scoring: 30-second epochs (standard criteria)
  3. Artifact removal:
    • Bad channels interpolated using spherical splines
    • ICA for ocular, muscular, cardiac artifacts
  4. Data extraction: 2 minutes before each awakening
  5. Re-referencing: Average reference

📈 Power Spectral Density (PSD) Analysis

Frequency Band Definition

Band Frequency Range Key Findings
Delta 1–4 Hz No significant effect on sleep depth
Theta 5–7.5 Hz No significant effect on sleep depth
Alpha 8–11.5 Hz Minimal effects
Sigma 12–16 Hz Predicted lighter sleep (good sleepers, NREM)
Beta 18–29.5 Hz Predicted lighter sleep (both groups, NREM & REM)
Gamma 30–45 Hz Predicted lighter sleep (misperceptors, REM)

PSD Computation

  • Method: Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)
  • Window: 2-second epochs, non-overlapping
  • Output: Absolute power (µV²)
  • Averaging: Across all 2-second epochs in the 120-second window

🧠 Source Localization (sLORETA)

Method Details

Forward Model

  • Individualized electrode coordinates per subject
  • Source space: 2,447 dipoles
  • Resolution: 7×7×7 mm cortical voxels
  • Restricted to cortical gray matter only

Inverse Solution

  • Method: standardized Low Resolution Electromagnetic Tomography
  • Regularization: Tikhonov (λ = 10⁻²)
  • Accounts for signal-to-noise variability

Key Findings at Source Level

Sigma & Beta power effects (NREM):

  • Spatially widespread across cortex
  • Peaked in central regions
  • Spared frontopolar and inferior temporal cortices

Beta & Gamma power effects (REM, misperceptors):

  • Even more widespread than good sleepers
  • Extended to frontopolar and occipital areas

🔄 Sleep Spindle Analysis

Detection Algorithm

  • Filtering: 11–16 Hz bandpass (3 dB at 10 and 17 Hz)
  • Downsampling: 128 Hz
  • Classification: Fast vs. slow spindles
    • Individualized threshold: mean of Fz and Pz frequencies
    • Good sleepers: 14.1 ± 0.6 Hz
    • Misperceptors: 14.4 ± 0.4 Hz

Extracted Parameters

  1. Overall density (spindles per minute)
  2. Fast spindle density
  3. Slow spindle density
  4. Maximal amplitude
  5. Duration
  6. Frequency

Key Findings

  • Higher spindle amplitude → lighter subjective sleep (both groups)
  • Higher fast spindle density → lighter subjective sleep (good sleepers only)
  • Overall spindle density: no significant effect
  • Effects were spatially widespread

📊 Statistical Analysis

Linear Mixed Models

Model Structure

Perceived Sleep Depth ~ PSD × Group + (1|Subject) + (1|Time_of_Night)

Components

  • Dependent variable: Perceived sleep depth (ordinal, 1-5 scale)
  • Fixed factors:
    • Power spectral density (or spindle parameter)
    • Group (good sleepers vs. misperceptors)
    • Interaction term (PSD × Group)
  • Random factors:
    • Subject identity (accounts for individual differences)
    • Time of night (hours after lights off)

Why Linear Mixed Models?

  • ✅ Handles repeated measures (multiple awakenings per subject)
  • ✅ Handles unbalanced data (different number of trials per subject)
  • ✅ Accounts for within-subject correlation
  • ✅ Models continuous predictors (EEG power values)
  • ✅ Allows missing data

Model Execution

  • Separate model for each electrode and each frequency band
  • Total: 256 electrodes × 6 frequency bands = 1,536 models (per sleep stage)
  • Ran models twice: once with each group as reference
  • Output statistic: Wald statistic (estimate² / SE²)

🎲 Multiple Comparison Correction

Cluster-Based Permutation Test

Step-by-Step Procedure

Step 1: Run Real Analysis

  • Compute linear mixed model for each electrode
  • Extract Wald statistics and p-values

Step 2: Build Null Distribution (1,000 iterations)

FOR each permutation (i = 1 to 1,000):
    1. SHUFFLE dependent variable labels WITHIN each subject
       (preserves subject structure)
    
    2. RUN mixed model on shuffled data for all electrodes
    
    3. IDENTIFY clusters:
       - Find electrodes with p < 0.05
       - Group spatially neighboring significant electrodes
    
    4. CALCULATE cluster statistic for each cluster:
       Cluster_Stat = Sum(Wald values in cluster) / N_electrodes
       (This is the AVERAGE Wald in the cluster)
    
    5. KEEP only the MAXIMUM absolute cluster statistic
       Max_Cluster_Stat[i] = max(|Cluster_Stat_1|, |Cluster_Stat_2|, ...)
    
END FOR

Result: Distribution of 1,000 maximum cluster statistics

Step 3: Set Significance Threshold

  • Threshold = 95th percentile of null distribution
  • Controls Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER) at α = 0.05

Step 4: Evaluate Real Clusters

  • Real clusters with statistics above threshold → significant
  • Mark significant electrodes with white dots in figures

Key Parameters

Parameter Value Rationale
Number of permutations 1,000 Computational feasibility with LMMs
Cluster-forming threshold p < 0.05 Balance sensitivity and specificity
Cluster statistic Average Wald Normalizes for cluster size
Permutation scheme Within-subject Preserves correlation structure
Significance level 95th percentile FWER = 0.05

What This Controls

Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER)

  • Probability of making ≥1 false positive across all 256 electrodes ≤ 5%
  • Very conservative approach
  • Strong control: protects against false positives everywhere
  • Appropriate for exploratory whole-brain analysis

🆚 Comparison with SnPM (Statistical non-Parametric Mapping)

Their Method

  • Model: Linear Mixed Model
  • Statistic: Wald statistic
  • Cluster stat: Average Wald
  • Threshold: p < 0.05
  • Permutations: 1,000
  • Scheme: Within-subject shuffling

Standard SnPM

  • Model: t-test / F-test
  • Statistic: t-value / F-value
  • Cluster stat: Sum of t-values (mass)
  • Threshold: Often p < 0.001
  • Permutations: 5,000-10,000
  • Scheme: Various (sign-flip, between/within)

Detailed Differences

Feature Their Method Standard SnPM Impact
Statistical Model Linear Mixed Model Simple t-test LMM properly handles repeated measures, random effects, unbalanced data
Cluster Statistic Sum(Wald) / N_electrodes Sum(t-values) or cluster size Averaging reduces bias toward large clusters
Threshold p < 0.05 (liberal) p < 0.001 (strict) Lower threshold may merge distinct effects
Permutation Scheme Within-subject shuffling Various options Within-subject appropriate for repeated measures
Computation Very intensive Fast Limits number of permutations (1,000 vs 10,000)
Flexibility High (covariates, interactions) Moderate Can model complex relationships

Similarities

  • ✅ Both use permutation testing
  • ✅ Both are non-parametric (no distribution assumptions)
  • ✅ Both control FWER (Family-Wise Error Rate)
  • ✅ Both use maximum statistic approach
  • ✅ Both handle mass univariate problems

Why Their Method is Better for This Study

  1. Multiple awakenings per subject (10-35 per person) → needs repeated measures
  2. Unbalanced design (different trials per subject) → LMM handles this
  3. Multiple random effects (subject, time) → LMM accounts for these
  4. Continuous predictors (EEG power predicting sleep depth) → regression framework needed
  5. Complex interactions (Group × EEG power) → LMM flexibility required

📌 Channel Selection for Analysis

Scalp-Level Analysis

Total channels: 256

Excluded channels:

  • ~4 electrodes near eyes (EOG monitoring)
  • Multiple electrodes over masseter/chin (EMG monitoring)
  • Electrodes on neck/face regions

Analyzed channels: ~248 cortical electrodes

The study restricted analysis to electrodes overlying cortical regions. This is evident from topographic maps showing typical head outline without face/neck coverage.

Source-Level Analysis

Explicitly restricted to cortex:

  • 2,447 dipoles in cortical gray matter only
  • 7×7×7 mm resolution
  • No subcortical structures modeled
  • Standard sLORETA approach

Clustering Approach

Spatial adjacency matrix:

  • Based on Euclidean distance on scalp
  • Used Electrical Geodesics standard electrode layout
  • Only cortical electrodes included in clustering
  • "Neighboring electrodes" defined by spatial proximity

🎯 Key Methodological Strengths

1. High-Density EEG

  • 256 channels provide excellent spatial sampling
  • Better source localization accuracy
  • Can detect focal effects

2. Serial Awakening Paradigm

  • Moment-to-moment assessment of sleep perception
  • 787 trials provide robust statistical power
  • Captures variability within and between subjects

3. Linear Mixed Models

  • Properly accounts for repeated measures
  • Handles unbalanced data
  • Models complex relationships (interactions, covariates)
  • More powerful than simple comparisons

4. Conservative Multiple Comparison Correction

  • FWER control protects against false positives
  • Cluster-based approach increases sensitivity
  • Non-parametric (no distribution assumptions)

5. Source Localization

  • Provides anatomical specificity
  • Reveals widespread cortical effects
  • Helps interpret scalp findings

⚠️ Limitations & Considerations

Computational Intensity

  • Only 1,000 permutations (vs. recommended 5,000-10,000)
  • Due to computational cost of LMMs
  • Still adequate for FWER control

Cluster-Forming Threshold

  • p < 0.05 is relatively liberal
  • May merge spatially distinct effects
  • Trade-off between sensitivity and specificity

Serial Awakening Effects

  • Sleep fragmentation alters sleep architecture
  • May affect subjective perception
  • Baseline night used to validate approach

Sample Size

  • Relatively small n = 10 sleep misperceptors
  • But large number of trials per subject
  • Mixed models handle this appropriately

💡 Practical Implications

For Sleep Research

  • Challenges conventional wisdom: Slow-wave sleep ≠ deepest subjective sleep
  • REM sleep associated with highest subjective depth
  • High-frequency activity (beta/gamma) as marker of arousal during sleep
  • Importance of moment-to-moment assessment vs. retrospective reports

For Insomnia Research

  • Sleep misperceptors show abnormal REM sleep perception
  • High-frequency EEG may explain subjective-objective discrepancy
  • Potential biomarker for treatment monitoring
  • Suggests misperceptors aren't "misperceiving" but detecting real neural signals

For EEG Methods

  • LMMs + permutation testing = powerful combination
  • Appropriate for complex repeated-measures designs
  • Balance between sensitivity and false positive control
  • High-density EEG provides spatial resolution comparable to fMRI (at scalp level)

🔑 Key Takeaways

Methodological Innovation

This study represents a sophisticated combination of:

  • High-density EEG (spatial resolution)
  • Serial awakening paradigm (temporal precision)
  • Linear mixed models (appropriate statistics)
  • Non-parametric permutation testing (robust inference)
  • Source localization (anatomical specificity)

Why Different from Standard SnPM

The approach is essentially "SnPM with Linear Mixed Models":

  • ✓ More appropriate for complex designs
  • ✓ More powerful (accounts for variance structure)
  • ✓ More flexible (covariates, interactions)
  • ⚠ More computationally intensive
  • ✓ Still maintains non-parametric control

Scientific Impact

  • Challenges the notion that slow-wave sleep = "deep sleep"
  • Identifies high-frequency EEG as marker of arousal during sleep
  • Validates sleep misperceptors' subjective experience
  • Opens new avenues for insomnia treatment
